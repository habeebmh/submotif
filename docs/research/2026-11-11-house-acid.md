# House bass composition research: hook, space, and articulation

Research date: 2026-11-11. Scope: Rolling, Offbeat, Deep, and Acid MIDI composition, with the user's taste references Mark Knight, Mau P, Chris Lake, and HUGEL. This is research and a proposed design direction, not a claim that the current engine implements these rules or that the artists endorse the product. No songs or MIDI patterns are copied.

## Main recommendation

Generate a fresh short musical idea, then develop it through repetition. Randomness should choose the idea's rhythm, pitch roles, contour, and articulation together. It should not repeatedly reinvent all four at every note. A note being in the selected scale does not establish a memorable phrase or make it fit an unknown chord progression.

Keep the six existing style choices. The artist references refine the desired character across those choices; they are not six genre definitions. Prioritize a compact, percussive, spacious tech-house hook for Rolling. Allow Deep and Funk to offer syncopated responses and deliberate gaps that leave room for percussion and vocals. Do not label any arbitrary syncopation “Latin,” or imply HUGEL's entire musical vocabulary is a bass rhythm formula.

## Composition evidence

**Repetition and controlled development.** Ableton's teaching analysis of [“Around the World”](https://learningmusic.ableton.com/make-basslines/around-the-world.html) highlights a contour repeated three times before a contrasting ending. This is a useful compositional principle, not a proposal to reuse the song's melody. Tim Cant's original [Native Instruments bass-writing tutorial](https://www.native-instruments.com/blogs/blog/how-to-write-a-bassline) builds from chord roots, adjusts an octave for consistent weight, adds syncopation and selected chord tones, then extends four bars to eight with one terminal sixteenth-note addition. It also explicitly keeps the bass straight despite swing in the drums. The lesson is that rhythm, register, harmonic role, and phrase development are considered separately and deliberately; “more movement” need not change every note.

**Rolling is not just more notes.** Toolroom's [production curriculum](https://toolroomacademy.com/programmes/interactive/production-certificate/) distinguishes rolling patterns, simple-note patterns, low sub parts, and melodic synth-led basslines within tech house. Tim Cant's [tech-house beat and bass tutorial for Loopmasters](https://www.loopmasters.com/articles/4300-How-To-Make-A-Tech-House-Beat-Bass-Tutorial) builds a complete drum groove, uses monophonic bass playback, and describes the ride as contributing roll. Its text alone does not specify the bass screenshot's exact rhythm. A separate [Production Music Live rolling-bass lesson](https://www.productionmusiclive.com/blogs/news/how-to-make-a-rolling-bassline) demonstrates repeated sixteenths and a recurring velocity contour, then adds selected octave and accent movement. That is a vendor's particular rolling synthesis example, not proof that all tech-house basslines should contain sixteen attacks per bar.

**Offbeat is a rhythmic profile.** An authored [MusicRadar kick/bass tutorial](https://www.musicradar.com/tutorials/music-production-tutorials/how-close-listening-to-chic-and-a-selection-of-other-artists-can-teach-us-about-the-integral-relationship-between-the-kick-and-bass) contrasts shared kick/bass accents with bass eighths between quarter-note kicks, using Robert Miles as the latter example. It shows those notes ending at the next kick. This supports an explicit offbeat option, not a new historical genre definition. Search results for “offbeat bass” often describe trance or psytrance; those should not silently define this project's house profile.

**Deep can be economical.** In his original [UNO Synth Pro sequencing tutorial](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/synth-secrets/3-quick-ways-to-program-basslines-on-an-uno-synth-pro/), Adam Douglas demonstrates a six-note deep-house line and reduces the sequence length instead of filling the default long sequence. Ableton's [“Never Tell You” lesson](https://learningmusic.ableton.com/make-basslines/never-tell-you.html) emphasizes empty space and notes between beats in a dub-techno example. The latter is adjacent evidence about space, not a definition of deep house. Neither source says that every Deep pattern must be sparse or use one fixed pitch collection.

**Acid articulation is structural.** Roland's [TB-303 sequencing lesson](https://articles.roland.com/mastering-the-tb-303-sequencer-in-roland-cloud/) treats pitch, gate, accent, and slide as separate pattern dimensions. It recommends contrast between selected slides and staccato notes, and warns that excessive accents lose impact; accents also affect the filter. The [Roland Cloud TB-303 manual](https://www.rolandcloud.com/getmedia/b3e2da28-1951-467e-bd79-7aae34481570/TB-303-Manual-E.pdf?ext=.pdf), printed pages 3 and 5, describes independent step data and a Modify operation that changes accent/slide/octave on existing gated steps, retaining rhythm and key. This is a particularly useful model for a restrained Variation action.

## Proposed algorithm: starting points to audition

Everything below is an engineering/composition proposal inferred from the evidence, not measured statistics about these artists. Numerical ranges are initial listening targets. They must not become hard-coded claims of authenticity.

### Shared phrase construction

1. Choose a one- or two-bar motif length independently of the requested output length. Sample a fresh rhythmic skeleton from style-specific musical roles and constraints, rather than retrieving a stored song pattern.
2. Give events roles: anchor, repeated pulse, answer, pickup, or optional ornament. Reserve a rest region as deliberately as an attack. Choose the pitch contour only after these roles exist.
3. Assign a small pitch vocabulary for the motif. The root and available stable scale tones can establish the center; approach notes resolve to a specified target. A random walk through all scale degrees is not the default.
4. Assign a recurring accent/gate contour. Repeated statements retain their phrase identity, including their rests. Humanization may add small deviations without changing strong/weak relationships.
5. Expand into a form such as A A A A′, or A B A B′ where B derives from A. A′ changes one or two ending events, not the entire bar. Do not add a fill at every boundary simply because the loop is long.
6. Generate makes a new motif. Variation normally changes one dimension or one ending while preserving the rest. Locks remain authoritative; motif copying must never silently rewrite locked notes.

With only key and scale supplied, the model knows a tonal center, not the track's chord progression. Favor a plausible tonic-centered phrase and describe it honestly. A future chord input could guide anchors/voice leading; it is not justified to invent a hidden progression now.

### Rolling: compact tech-house hook

- Prefer a one-bar core with a related second-bar answer. Start around 5–9 attacks per bar at the default density, with quieter repeat pulses around a recognizable anchor/answer. A high-energy sixteenth stream can be a less frequent alternative, not the defining default.
- Choose 1–3 pitch classes per core motif at normal movement; repeat pitches often. Use the chosen bass register consistently. An octave reply belongs at a repeatable phrase location rather than being a separate coin flip for each note.
- Establish one short gate family for pulses and one longer anchor/answer family. Repeat the velocity contour. Leave at least one meaningful gap; adding density should decorate the hook before filling that gap.
- A different bass sound, envelope, sidechain setting, or pitch bend may change perceived roll dramatically. Ordinary notes, velocity, and durations cannot guarantee those audio behaviors.

### Offbeat: preserve the “and”

- Build the main pulse around beat offsets 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5 in a four-beat bar. At low density omit selected pulses consistently; at high density add a small pickup or response while preserving the offbeat backbone.
- Keep pitch changes infrequent and phrase-aligned. Equal or closely related gates make the pulse legible; a useful default ends each main note before the next quarter-note kick.
- Do not force a downbeat note merely to satisfy an “anchor root” toggle. Anchoring concerns pitch function; the first structurally strong offbeat can carry the root. Do not let maximum density turn this into undifferentiated sixteenths.

### Deep: low anchor, measured response, room to breathe

- Begin around 3–6 attacks per bar, often with one sustained anchor and a shorter syncopated response. Try an anticipated arrival or a recurring unequal subdivision grouping as a complete cell, rather than scattering isolated syncopations.
- Keep adjacent structural notes in a compact register. At moderate movement, let a 2–4 pitch vocabulary and a repeated contour do more work than frequent octave leaps. Selected scale colors should have a destination.
- Reuse the call/answer and change its final gesture only at a larger phrase boundary. For the HUGEL-facing taste direction, audition more empty space and percussion-aware responses in Deep/Funk; do not claim the bass alone recreates a Latin ensemble or impose a universal clave.

### Acid: repeat the cell, vary articulation

- Start with an 8- or 16-step cell. Compose its gate/rest pattern and compact pitch contour separately, then repeat both. Vary selected accents or a small ending before increasing pitch randomness.
- Give rests, short gates, and sustained connections different roles. Optional octave movement should belong to the repeated cell. Sparse contrasting accents communicate more than marking every attack strong.
- The current plain-note output can suggest acid sequencing but cannot promise authentic 303 slide/accent behavior. Velocity needs a compatible destination mapping; portamento/legato depends on the receiving synth and may require overlaps or a specific protocol. Treat native slide flags/overlap export as a separate design decision, not an invisible reinterpretation of the Gate control.

## Control meaning and evaluation

| Control | Proposed musical meaning | Failure to guard against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Density | Add/remove optional events around a retained skeleton; repeat the resulting choice with the motif. | Destroying rests and style identity at high values. |
| Movement | Increase the number of deliberate pitch roles, contour span, or phrase response changes. | Independent pitch randomness on every attack. |
| Gate | Scale articulation families while respecting required separation and locked notes. | A single arbitrary duration distribution obscuring the groove. |
| Swing | Apply a consistent subdivision relationship to eligible events. | Confusing syncopation with swing or applying unrelated timing jitter. |
| Root anchor | Keep structurally important pitch arrivals tonic-centered. | Inserting an unwanted downbeat in every style. |
| Octave jumps | Permit intentional octave replies and repeat them coherently. | Breaking the bass register at unrelated points. |

For audition, use the same neutral bass patch, tempo, kick, and percussion with old/new generated clips; then repeat with the user's intended instrument. Listen for a phrase that can be remembered after two repetitions, a clear relationship to the kick, preserved gaps, and a recognizable style when density changes. Include root-only cases: a good rhythmic hook should survive with one pitch. Compare at least several seeds per style rather than selecting one attractive demo.

Useful diagnostics include repeated-onset agreement at the chosen motif lag, pitch-class count, repeated accent/gate contour, interval distribution, and where changes occur in the phrase. These detect regressions but cannot certify that a line grooves. Preserve the existing validity, lock, export, playback, and persistence tests alongside listening checks.

## Research limits

- These are original interviews, vendor educational material, and authored demonstrations/analyses. A tutorial illustrates a technique; it does not establish a statistical rule for a genre or artist.
- No exact artist basslines were transcribed in this research. The proposed note counts, forms, and limits are our design choices. Artist names describe taste direction, not a preset that reproduces an artist.
- The source distinction between pattern and sound is essential. Filter envelopes, resonance, distortion, sidechain compression, sub/mid layering, and synth glide are not MIDI note-composition features.
- Roland's sequencing article suggests a 13-step loop never returns to the same metrical position. That wording is mathematically incorrect: against a 16-step bar it realigns after 208 sixteenths (13 bars, or 16 repetitions of the 13-step cell). Odd lengths are optional polymetric experiments, not a necessary acid rule or a reason to break requested loop length.
- “Deep,” “Rolling,” and “Funk” overlap in actual records. Preserve audible profile differences without claiming that their boundaries are universal.
